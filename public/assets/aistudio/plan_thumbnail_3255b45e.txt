## 2. Prompt engineering and high-CTR design

### The seven ingredients of a strong thumbnail prompt

1\. Narrative specificity

Name the actual protagonist, threat, setting, and stakes. If the story is about a medieval zombie outbreak, show medieval survivors and the established zombie threat—not generic people traveling through a dark landscape.

2\. One curiosity-driving visual idea

Show an instantly understandable question: Can this survivor escape? What happened on day 60? Why is the castle overrun? The image should suggest a story without explaining the entire plot.

3\. Character identity preservation

Reuse the project's character descriptions and reference images. Preserve distinguishing facial features, hairstyle, clothing, age, body proportions, and established design language.

4\. Deliberate composition

Choose a dominant subject, a secondary story element, a readable silhouette, and a clearly reserved area for the headline. Do not let the background compete with the face or action.

5\. Controlled cinematic lighting

Use a strong key light, directional rim lighting where appropriate, face illumination, and separation between the subject and background. Preserve the project's original aesthetic rather than imposing the same orange-and-teal look on every project.

6\. Deliberate typography

Use one short, exact headline, a simple heavy typeface, strong contrast, and a clearly specified location. Typography is part of the composition, not an afterthought.

7\. Negative-space and clutter control

Specify a clean focal area, distinct subject-background separation, and uncluttered text space. Avoid piling on unrelated props, extra characters, multiple headlines, and decorative effects.

### Character consistency versus dramatic lighting

This is a particularly important design problem for StoryFrame because you already have a character continuity system.

The correct principle is: lock identity; vary presentation.

Identity attributes should be inherited as constraints, while expression, pose, lighting, framing, and emotional intensity can be changed to suit the thumbnail.

For example:

- Locked: scar over the left eyebrow, shoulder-length dark hair, battered medieval armor, established low-poly horror style.
- Adjustable: widened eyes, clenched jaw, three-quarter angle, stronger rim light, closer framing.
- Story-dependent: whether the character looks terrified, determined, exhausted, or victorious.

Do not use “make the character more dramatic” as an instruction to redesign their face, costume, or art style.

Also, text-to-image prompting alone cannot guarantee exact identity preservation. If the downstream image model supports reference images, pass the existing character reference where possible. A description alone is a weaker identity anchor than an appropriate visual reference.

## 4. Potential pitfalls and recommendations

These are the mistakes I would prioritize during implementation and testing.

Pitfall 1: Choosing the final scene automatically

The ending may be quiet, anticlimactic, or a resolution that gives away too much. Choose the most compelling story-supported visual moment, which may come from the climax, a turning point, or an unanswered question.

Pitfall 2: Generic cinematic language

Words like “epic,” “ultra-detailed,” and “dramatic” are not a substitute for story understanding. Explicitly identify what the character is doing, what threatens them, and why the moment matters.

Pitfall 3: Style drift

Do not append a generic photorealistic movie-poster style to every project. A stylized low-poly story, an illustrated mystery, and a cinematic live-action thriller need different lighting, textures, and material descriptions.

Pitfall 4: Overloading the prompt

Excessive details can create competing instructions. Prioritize the focal subject, story conflict, style, composition, lighting, and typography. Keep secondary props only when they reinforce the narrative.

Pitfall 5: Forcing text rendering

A model may misspell the headline, invent additional lettering, or produce malformed typography. Keep the exact headline in a dedicated field and make text-safe artwork or external typography a future-compatible option.

Pitfall 6: Misleading clickbait

Do not introduce a giant monster, an apocalypse, a death, or a survival outcome that never happens in the story. YouTube's own guidance emphasizes accurate titles and thumbnails.&#x20;

The template above is the logical specification. In production, compile only the relevant information into the actual request.

```ts
    visualIdentity: string;
  };
  styleProfile: StyleProfile;
  candidateTextHooks: string[];
}
```

Generation pipeline:

1. Derive the brief locally from the existing project state.
2. Ask the LLM for a compact JSON response: `visualConcept`, `composition`, `lighting`, `expression`, `primaryText`, `secondaryText`, and `placement`.
3. Compile the final image prompt deterministically in the client.
4. Store only the final compact `ThumbnailPrompt`, not the full model reasoning or full manuscript.

This keeps free-tier usage low because the model receives a few hundred tokens of structured context and returns a few hundred tokens of structured output.

## 2. High-CTR prompt engineering

### Essential ingredients

A high-CTR thumbnail needs more than a beautiful frame. It needs one readable story in under one second:

- **One hero subject:** A face, object, or unmistakable visual icon.
- **One clear emotion:** Fear, triumph, shock, determination, curiosity, or desperation.
- **One moment of conflict:** The visual should imply a question, not summarize the ending.
- **Extreme readability:** High subject-background separation, simplified background, strong rim or key light.
- **One text idea:** Usually 2–5 words, never a sentence.
- **Platform-safe composition:** Critical elements must survive UI overlays and cropping.
- **Style continuity:** Same character wardrobe, art direction, palette, and world aesthetic as the storyboard.

For Midjourney and Flux, front-load the subject and action, then environment, lighting, style, and technical specifications. This ordering is specifically recommended for Flux prompting. ### Character lock versus thumbnail lighting

Do not let “thumbnail lighting” override the project’s locked identity. Separate the layers:

```text
Character identity lock:
[exact face, age, skin tone, hair, eye color, wardrobe, distinguishing features]

Thumbnail lighting adaptation:
Dramatic rim light from behind left, strong key light on face,
high contrast, deep shadows, simplified background.
```

The character’s physical identity stays fixed; only the lighting, expression, framing, and background are adapted for thumbnail impact. This follows StoryFrame’s existing separation between persistent identity sheets and beat-level visual direction. 

## 3. 9:16 versus 16:9

### 16:9 YouTube thumbnail

Design at 1280 × 720 or larger, maintaining 16:9. Keep critical text and the face inside roughly the central 80 percent of the frame, with extra clearance in the bottom-right for the duration badge and along the bottom edge for the progress bar. [runware](https://runware.ai/docs/models/bfl-flux-3-image/guides/text-rendering)

Recommended composition:

```text
Hero face or subject: center-left or right third
Primary text: upper-left, center-left, or center
Avoid: bottom-right corner, bottom 10-15 percent, extreme edges
Text size: bold enough to remain readable at small feed sizes
```

A practical safe envelope on a 1280 × 720 canvas is approximately x 80–1020 and y 60–600, with the bottom-right timestamp zone kept free of text and focal facial features. [runware](https://runware.ai/docs/models/bfl-flux-3-image/guides/text-rendering)

### 9:16 TikTok, Reels, and Shorts cover

Design at 1080 × 1920. The major constraint is vertical UI: profile and navigation occupy the top, captions and sound attribution occupy the bottom, and engagement buttons occupy the right side. One current safe-zone guide places usable content between y 290 and y 1540 and x 40 and x 1000 on a 1080 × 1920 canvas. [thumbsignal](https://thumbsignal.com/en/blog/youtube-thumbnail-safe-zones-where-text-gets-cut)

Recommended composition:

```text
Hero face or subject: upper-center to center
Primary text: center or upper-center
Avoid: bottom third, right-side button column, extreme top and bottom
Text width: no more than about 70-80 percent of frame width
```

For TikTok profile grids, covers may be cropped from 9:16 toward roughly 3:4, cutting material from the top and bottom. Keep the face and headline near the vertical center, not near either edge. [thumbsignal](https://thumbsignal.com/en/blog/youtube-thumbnail-safe-zones-where-text-gets-cut)

## 4. Pitfalls and final template

### Pitfalls to avoid

1. **Treating the thumbnail as a story beat.** It should not inherit a beat duration, narration line, or timeline position.
2. **Letting the LLM write final prompt syntax.** The LLM should choose concept, subject, emotion, and text; deterministic code should compile the prompt.
3. **Using long overlay text.** “ZOMBIE OUTBREAK FOR 60 DAYS” is already near the upper limit. Prefer `DAY 60: SURVIVED` or `60 DAYS INSIDE`.
4. **Placing text over the hero’s face.** Preserve facial readability; use negative space beside or above the subject.
5. **Ignoring platform safe zones.** A beautiful bottom-right headline on YouTube can be covered by the duration badge. [runware](https://runware.ai/docs/models/bfl-flux-3-image/guides/text-rendering)
6. **Breaking style continuity.** A photorealistic thumbnail on a rubber-hose storyboard will feel like a different product.
7. **Generating only one option.** Offer three variants: face-led, object-led, and situation-led.
8. **Assuming Flux and Midjourney behave identically.** Flux responds well to quoted text and explicit typography; Midjourney needs more conservative text handling and may be better used with text added in a design layer. ## 1. Placement and schema

- **Placement:** Keep it between the last scene and the narrator script in the document and in the MD/JSON/CSV exports. Creators usually want it first, though, so add a "Cover" chip or jump link at the top of results. Style the card differently from scene cards.
- **Not a beat:** Keep it out of beat counts, WPM pacing, SRT and EDL.
- **Token-cheap schema:** Have the model return short fields only, and assemble the final prompt in code. That also keeps Groq's smaller models from drifting. Generate it in the same call as the story bible, or in a small follow-up that sees only the bible summary, never the full story.

```json
"thumbnail": {
  "heroMoment": "one line, peak tension, not the resolution",
  "expression": "3-6 words",
  "bgDescription": "dark, simplified, 6-10 words",
  "accentColor": "one color",
  "hooks": ["≤4 words", "alt 1", "alt 2"]
}
```

Code adds the aspect ratio, the style lock, the character lock and the text phrasing. The alternate hooks give creators free A/B options.

## 2. High-CTR ingredients

- One subject, one emotion, one saturated accent color against a dark, simple background.
- Subject fills 40-60% of the frame, with rim light and exaggerated expression.
- Reserved negative space for the text.
- It must read at about 170px wide.

**Text phrasing:**
- Use 4 words or fewer.
- Put it in quotes, in ALL CAPS.
- Name the typography ("bold heavy sans-serif, thick black outline").
- Name the placement.
- State it once, and add "no other text".
- Flux.1 handles this well. Midjourney v6.1 is decent but fails on long strings.
- Offer a "text-free plate" toggle that leaves clean negative space, since many creators add text in Canva anyway.

**Locked character vs. dramatic lighting:** Keep the identity tokens verbatim (face, hair, outfit, marks). Let only expression, lighting and camera vary. Write "same character design and outfit, new lighting" and don't re-describe the wardrobe.

## 3. 9:16 vs 16:9

These zones are approximate, so verify them against current platform templates.

- **16:9:** Keep the bottom-right clear for the timestamp. Put the subject on one third and the text on the opposite side. Use a single line of text.
- **9:16:** Keep the top ~12% and bottom ~25% free of key content, plus the right edge for action buttons. Put the face and text in the central band. Profile grids crop to about 3:4, so keep key content inside that too. Stack the text on 2 lines, larger.

## 4. Pitfalls

- **Conflict with your no-text-in-images rule:** Scope the exception to the exact hook string only, or the generator will print narration as subtitles again.
- **Invented or over-promised details:** The hook and frame must be supported by the story bible. Don't promise what the video doesn't deliver.
- **Generic frame for abstract stories:** Anchor it in the bible's specific character and setting.
- **Copying the final scene:** Require a different composition, and show the climax, not the resolution.
- **No real people's names:** Keep the existing rule.
- **Weak hook validation:** Check it in code (≤4 words, uppercase, no stray punctuation).

## Final template (assembled in code)

```
{STYLE_LOCK}. {CHARACTER_LOCK}, same design and outfit, {expression}, {heroMoment}. Tight medium close-up, subject fills about 50% of frame, placed {left/right third}. Strong {accentColor} rim light from behind, deep shadows, high contrast, dark simplified background: {bgDescription}. Clean negative space {opposite side} for text. Text: the words "{HOOK}" in bold heavy white sans-serif capitals with thick black outline, {placement}, appears exactly once, perfectly spelled, no other text, no logos, no watermark.
```

- **Midjourney:** append `--ar {16:9|9:16} --style raw --s 150 --v 6.1`
- **Flux.1:** no parameters. Add the orientation as a sentence ("vertical 9:16 composition, keep the top and bottom 20% free of key content").
